MILESTONE 1 · PROBLEM STATEMENT

Differential: simulation-grounded AI diagnosis for analog
audio electronics
PRV 201 Frontiers of Technology (AI) · Track A: Build · Stevens Institute of Technology · September 2026


Executive summary. When a tube preamplifier, studio console or vintage hi-fi unit fails, a technician must find which
of its dozens to hundreds of parts failed; specialist shops we sampled charge $125 to $150 for that evaluation [1, 2, 3].
Differential assists the search. For a circuit it has a model of (Figure 1), it simulates how every cataloged part failure
changes the readings, keeps a probability for every suspect and recommends the measurement expected to narrow them
most per unit of effort; a language model reads the complaint and explains but never supplies a reading, and safety rules
run in code. Success, fixed before any test unit is run, means naming the failed part first on at least 90% of 1,000 simulated
units and on at least 49 of 60 faults inserted into real circuit boards, beating the best scripted procedure and a chat model,
using at most 0.35 times an expert’s probing effort, stating honest probabilities, and sending at most 11% of units outside
its fault list to a healthy part. All evidence so far is simulated; the boards and a technician study will test transfer and real
benefit. Scope: single static faults in preamplifier and line-level circuits.

  Part 1 — Problem, stakeholders and industry context                                                        Section lead: [name removed]

The problem. Studio consoles, tube preamplifiers and vintage hi-fi are repaired long after their makers stop supporting
them, and finding the failed part is usually the slow step: for heavy equipment, a trade article puts diagnosing an electrical
fault at four hours or more against a ten-minute repair [4]; for audio, our interviews will measure it. Three specialist shops
we sampled charge $125 to $150 for a bench evaluation, one covering the first hour of repair and one credited against it
[1, 2, 3]; another bills a one-hour minimum even when no fault is found [5]. These are examples, not a survey.
A small, specialized workforce. The closest U.S. occupation, audiovisual equipment installers and repairers (24,720
people in May 2023 [6]), is projected to grow faster than average [7] and is not old (median age 38.4 in 2017 [8]); the
scarcity is narrower, as a 2026 trade report describes informally trained vintage-audio specialists, many near retirement,
and repair queues of months [9]. An AI assistant raised support agents’ productivity 14% on average and 34% for novices
[10]; if that holds at the bench, newer technicians gain most, so the tool must support their judgment, not replace it.
Where it pays. A new circuit needs its schematic transcribed (we will time one) and, for our 59-part channel strip, about
75 minutes of computer time; that is repaid fastest where one model serves many units, such as a console of identical
channel strips or a popular receiver.
Where current AI stands. Maintenance AI retrieves documented knowledge: service histories (PTC ServiceMax AI [11]),
manuals (Microsoft’s field-service Copilot [12]), predictive-maintenance guidance (Siemens [13]), agentic workflows (IBM

Rules that apply. The EU AI Act would not class a bench aid as high-risk: no Annex I law requires it to pass third-party

[20] and keep a duty to support staff’s AI literacy [21], which our training material serves.

  DESIGN DECISION · The primary user is a qualified bench technician
  Options: a consumer self-repair assistant or a professional bench tool. Choice: professional, because diagnosis often means
  testing a powered, open chassis. OSHA’s rules, which protect employees, require live parts to be de-energized unless testing
  needs power (parts under 50 V to ground excepted, if that adds no burn or arc-blast exposure) and let only qualified persons,
  trained in the equipment and its hazards, work on energized parts [22, 23] or test circuits [24]; 5 of the channel strip’s 23
  test points can exceed 50 V (Table 2). Consequence: licensed to repair businesses for qualified technicians; trainees work
  supervised; mains-side work is refused. Hobbyists keep forums and chat models, which have no safety rules.


  Part 2 — How diagnosis is done today, and the data landscape                                             Section lead: [name removed]

Today’s practice. The usual sequence, which our interviews will check: confirm the complaint; look for burnt or bulging
parts; check the supplies; follow a test tone stage by stage, often halving the search (“half-split tracing”); compare voltages
with the manual’s chart or the healthy channel; test tubes and electrolytics with a tube tester or ESR meter; check service
bulletins; then substitute or unsolder a suspect and confirm the fault clears. The hard judgment is which reading is really
“off”, since parts sit anywhere within tolerance and old ones drift beyond it: drying electrolytics lose capacitance and gain
series resistance [25]. Replacing every electrolytic (“recapping”) never finds the failed part.
The information is uneven. Service manuals with schematics and voltage charts exist for many classic models, but
units are often modified or undocumented. U.S. state repair laws cover only products first sold after 2015 or 2021, several
only digital electronics [26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31], and the EU’s obligation to repair covers only the product groups in its
Annex II, such as washing machines and displays [32, 33]: vintage analog gear falls outside all of them. Differential needs
a circuit model per product, built from the schematic, and trusts its hazard map only once the model’s healthy readings
match the manual’s chart. Modified units are what “no single fault fits” is for; units without a schematic are out of scope.
Labeled fault data is scarce, so we simulate it. Confirming a failed part takes unsoldering and is rarely recorded; shop
logs are private and, after recapping, unlabeled; manuals give only healthy values. We know of no public dataset of bench
readings with confirmed failed parts; published methods use benchmark filter circuits [34] and simulated data [35]. The
analog-test literature built simulated fault dictionaries [36], showed that some faults cannot be told apart at the available
test points, forming ambiguity groups [37], and applied machine learning [34, 38] to drift faults that tolerances hide [35];
we follow that path (Table 2). A simulated unit is one draw of every part within its tolerance, the mains within ±5%, and
one cataloged fault (an open, a short, a drift, or a part-specific mode such as a leaky transistor or a worn tube), and every
reading carries a real instrument’s error: a 10 MΩ meter loads each DC node [39] and reads within ±(0.5% + 2 counts)
[40], an oscilloscope within ±3% [41]. The library gave 253,000 units, 0 failing to converge. Hand calculations match every
simulated bias point and gain within 0.5-5% or 0.1 dB (22 of 22 checks). Fault behavior has not been checked fault by fault
(one close look exposed a simulator artifact, since fixed), so real boards are the systematic check. A complaint alone is
weak evidence: on the channel strip a hum complaint fits 25 faults, low output 21, and 119 faults usually cause none.

  Part 3 — Objectives and measurable success criteria                                                           Section lead: [name removed]

Objectives. (O1) Name the failed part or its ambiguity group accurately, (O2) with less measurement effort than scripted
troubleshooting, (O3) honestly: calibrated probabilities, visible ambiguity groups and an explicit “no single fault fits”; (O4)
safely: no unsafe step or invented reading; (O5) practically: fast, cheap, reading plain complaints and meter photos while
the technician’s hands are on the probes. T1-T7 test O1, T8-T11 O2, T12-T14 and T22-T23 O3, T15-T17 O4, T18-T21 O5.
Effort units. A DC reading costs 1, an oscilloscope measurement 3, a point that can exceed 50 V 2 more (hands-off probing
is slower) and unsoldering a part 10, within a budget of 40. These are estimates: every effort comparison is repeated with
each of the last three weights halved and doubled, and the boards will time the low-voltage ones.
How it will be measured. The test set holds 1,000 single-fault units (500 on the channel strip, 100 per block), seeds
disjoint from training. Each case draws a fault uniformly from the catalog and keeps the unit only if it shows a symptom,
else draws another fault, so faults count by how often they cause a symptom, not how often they fail; old electrolytics
and tubes fail far more often than film resistors, so T14 also reweights to a capacitor-heavy mix. Complaints come from
a phrase bank the reader was not developed on (its overall score was seen once, before the rules last changed; T18 uses
a sealed second bank), in three voices, never name a part, and drop each symptom with probability 20% and add each
other symptom with 2%. Stress sets: 500 units outside the catalog (70% double faults, 30% a wrong-value part or a solder
bridge); 300 aged units (electrolytics up to 30% low with up to 3× their series resistance, resistors up to 10% high, tubes
up to 30% weaker); tolerances 1.5, 2 and 3 times wider. The scripts, both engines and the full system (Table 4) diagnose
every unit; the scripts share the engine’s inference and stopping rule and unsolder their leading suspect at 50% belief, so
each margin isolates how measurements are chosen. A language model works alone, and with our simulator as a tool (no
bar), on a stratified 300-unit subset with the same information, three sessions per unit.
Hardware and people. For T7 a teammate who does not record inserts 60 faults, in a sealed random order, into three 24
V driver boards (one of used parts); each test point is read once per fault and every method replayed on the recordings.
Study S1 (pre-registered, no bar) will time at least six technicians with and without the tool, after ethics review.
Pilots set the bars; no test unit has been run. Bars come from a pilot on units simulated for it alone (200 channel strip,
60 per block, 250 out-of-model, 150 aged), after two earlier pilots exposed problems since fixed. Weighted like the test set
(channel strip one half, each block one tenth), the full system named the right group first on 92.1% of pilot units, naming
a group on 93.2% and right on 98.8% of those. The engine alone, ignoring the complaint, was right on 93.8%: the noisy
complaints saved 4.8% of the effort but cost 1.8 points of accuracy, so the complaint reader has yet to prove itself (T11,
T18). On the channel strip the engine alone was right on 94.0% at a mean effort of 6.7, half-split tracing on 83.5% at 22.9,
the chart on 77.0% at 24.1 and random probing on 80.5% at 23.0. Each bar is the pilot estimate moved 1.5 standard errors
against the system and rounded; because the confidence bound must clear it, a primary can be missed even if the system
matches the pilot, and a miss will be reported as one.
Scope. In: single static faults (opens, shorts, drift, wear) and the hum and distortion they cause, in supplies, preamplifier
and line-level stages. Out: power amplifiers (another catalog), intermittent and mechanical faults (static simulation cannot
produce them), double faults (flagged only), mains-side work, units without a schematic, unattended probing.

  DESIGN DECISION · Score by ambiguity group and weighted effort
  Options: per-part accuracy and a count of measurements, or group accuracy and weighted effort. Choice: the latter: per-
  part scoring rewards lucky guesses between faults with identical readings, and unsoldering a part is not like touching a probe.
  Consequence: group sizes are reported (Table 2) and effort targets use weighted units.


  Part 4 — Preliminary AI approach, risks and ethics                                                               Section lead: [name removed]

Why a hybrid. Language models guess when uncertain, partly because training and evaluation reward guessing [42];
the 97.59% system in Part 1 — Problem, stakeholders and industry context needed a simulation check. Simulation supplies
the physics; Bayesian inference keeps a probability for every suspect, “no single fault fits” included, updated after each
reading through likelihoods learned from the simulated units: one to three Gaussians per fault over the circuit’s readings
(51 on the channel strip, 23 of them DC voltages), with boosted trees [43] as a comparison engine (weighted pilot top-1:
93.8% and 92.8%). The next measurement maximizes expected information gain per unit of effort, as in sequential fault
diagnosis [44]; a diagnosis stops at 90% belief in one group, at the budget, or when nothing informative remains. The
language model reads complaints (offline, a rule-based reader does), proposes meter-photo readings for confirmation and
explains each step; every number it writes must trace to a reading (T17). It stays only if T5, T18 and T19 show it helps.
Autonomy. On a five-level scale we define by analogy to driving automation, from L0 (the technician does everything) to
L4 (a test fixture diagnoses unattended), Differential sits at L2: it recommends measurements and names suspects, while
the technician chooses, takes and confirms every measurement. It never acts on a unit alone, because a wrong step near
high voltage can injure a person and each measurement must stay a qualified person’s decision.
Safety. A fault can put high voltage where there is normally none: channel-strip test point TP10, normally about 0 V,
reaches 125 V under “C203 short circuit”. Steps at points that can exceed 50 V carry hands-off and discharge instructions;
in a chassis with a high-voltage supply every powered step says “high voltage inside”. Before a part is removed, the
owner’s approval is recorded and the technician must record under 2 V at every point that can hold high voltage, catching

Over-trust, deskilling and people. Runner-up suspects and reasons are shown, but explanations can raise trust in
wrong advice: consultants using AI were 19 points less likely to solve a task outside its competence [46], and confidence
in AI goes with less critical thinking [47]. In trainee mode the tool shows its next step only after the trainee records their
own, and high-voltage units need a named supervisor; a tutor giving hints avoided the learning loss an unrestricted chat
model caused [48]. The technician study plants one wrong recommendation, on a non-hazardous step of a 24 V board,
only after an ethics-board determination, with consent that discloses it and a debrief. As fairness audits do for groups of
people [49], errors will be broken down by who bears them: complaint writers (regional terms such as “valve”), novice
and experienced technicians, and owners of undocumented gear, whom the scope excludes.
Accountability and liability. OSHA’s rules protect employees and so bind the shop. Whether advice software is a
product (strict liability) or a service (negligence) is unsettled in the U.S.: the proposed AI LEAD Act would make AI
systems products [50], and the proposed RISE Act would shield developers who publish a model card and specification
only when a licensed or certified professional uses the tool [51], which a bench technician may not be. The revised EU
Product Liability Directive makes software a product from 9 December 2026 and covers damage to consumer property
such as a customer’s hi-fi [52]; under the AI Act the tool owes transparency and AI-literacy duties, not high-risk ones
(Part 1 — Problem, stakeholders and industry context), unless shops rate staff with it. The team answers for the hazard
map, stated performance and corrections; the shop for its users and circuit models; the technician for measurements and
precautions.
  DESIGN DECISION · Safety and security rules live in code, not only in the prompt
  Options: instruct the model to be safe, or enforce safety in code. Choice: code: prompt injection ranks first in the OWASP
  Top 10 for LLM applications [54] and injected text can steer a model from its instructions [55], so notes, complaints and photo
  text are data and the model’s tools act only on the session. Consequence: warnings, refusals and locks behave the same in
  every mode; T15 checks them at every step.
