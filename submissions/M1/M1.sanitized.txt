MILESTONE 1 · PROBLEM STATEMENT

Differential: simulation-grounded AI diagnosis for analog
audio electronics
PRV 201 Frontiers of Technology (AI) · Track A: Build · Stevens Institute of Technology · September 2026


Executive summary. When a tube preamplifier, studio console or vintage hi-fi unit fails, a technician must find which
of its many parts failed, and specialist shops charge for that bench evaluation. Differential simulates how every cataloged
part failure changes a modeled circuit’s readings (Figure 1), keeps a probability for every group of suspects that readings
cannot tell apart, and recommends the measurement expected to narrow them most per unit of effort; a language model
reads the complaint and explains; the technician takes every reading and safety record. In a simulated pilot it named
the failed part’s group first on 95.6% of as-new and 93.2% of aged units, with 0.65 times the effort of a scripted half-split
search using the same inference; no hardware result exists yet. The locked primary targets (Table 3) include 4,728 of 5,000
simulated units right, 4,584 of 5,000 aged and 49 of 60 faults on real 24 V boards; each will be reported met or missed.




Figure 1. Offline, each circuit’s faults are simulated with SPICE, a circuit simulator, under part tolerances, as-new and aged, and learned as
      fault signatures. Online, a Bayesian loop picks each measurement; a language model (LLM) reads the complaint and explains.


  Part 1 — Problem, stakeholders and industry context                                                            Section lead: [name removed]

The problem. Studio consoles, tube preamplifiers and vintage hi-fi are repaired long after their makers stop supporting
them, and finding the failed part is the slow step: U.S. defense maintainability guidance holds that most repair time
typically goes to fault isolation [1], a premise our ticket coding will test for audio benches. Differential, proposed here,
helps a qualified bench technician in an independent shop or a studio find the part: it simulates the unit’s circuit to rank
the parts that could have failed and choose each next measurement. Specialist shops we sampled charge $125 to $150 for
a bench evaluation [2, 3, 4], and one bills an hour even when no fault is found [5]; these are examples, not a survey.
Size of the need. No statistic counts audio repair: it fell under consumer electronics repair, a U.S. industry code merged
into a broader one in 2022 [6], and the closest occupation (24,720 people in May 2023) works mainly in retail and
construction [7, 8]. The broader electronics repair group (117,700 jobs in 2025) is projected to grow 2% to 2035, below
the 3.5% average [9, 10]. Only press reports cover the niche: skilled hi-fi technicians “are scarce, and their numbers are
dwindling” [11], and a Chicago shop for old audio gear shrank from seven technicians to two [12]. Where it pays: a
model needs the schematic transcribed (we will time one) and, for our 59-part channel strip, 81 minutes of computer time,
so it pays where one model serves many units, such as a console of identical channel strips, once minutes saved per job,
times jobs, exceed the modeling time (S1 and the tickets will measure both).
Where current AI stands. Maintenance AI works from records and documents (service histories [13], manuals [14],
repair guidance with failure prediction [15], agentic workflows [16], repair guides [17]); none of these five simulates the
circuit. On one leaderboard the top chat model scores 95.5% on textbook analog-design questions, graded by a language
model [18], but at the bench parts sit anywhere within tolerance, faults look alike, measurements cost effort and some
points carry lethal voltage. A pipeline adding a circuit-diagram detector and a simulator check to a language model lifted it
from 79.52% to 97.59% on undergraduate circuit problems [19]; our model-with-simulator baseline tests that idea (Part 3).
Jurisdiction and rules. We design for the U.S. and check EU rules for later sale. No federal AI statute regulates such
a tool, but the FTC has acted against unsupported AI accuracy claims [20]. Texas’s AI act (in force since 2026) binds it

  Part 2 — How diagnosis is done today, and the data landscape                                             Section lead: [name removed]

Today’s practice (the interviews will check it): with a multimeter, an oscilloscope, a signal generator and a variac or
series lamp [32, 33], a technician confirms the complaint; looks for burnt or bulging parts; checks the supplies; follows
a test tone stage by stage, often halving the search (“half-split tracing”); compares readings with the manual’s voltage
chart or the healthy channel [34]; tests tubes and electrolytics (tube tester, ESR meter); unsolders a suspect and confirms
the fault clears. In our reading, the time goes to judging which reading is really “off”, since parts sit anywhere within
tolerance and aging electrolytics lose capacitance and gain series resistance [35], and to unsoldering suspects that prove
good; the ticket coding (Part 1) will measure it. Replacing every electrolytic (“recapping”) often cures a symptom but costs
parts and time, risks the board and never names the failed part.
The information is uneven. Manuals with schematics and voltage charts exist for many classic models, but units are
often modified or undocumented. U.S. repair laws cover newer digital products, not vintage analog gear: New York’s took
effect in December 2023 [36, 37], and six other states followed with limits by date or product [38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43]; the
EU’s obligation to repair covers only its Annex II products, such as washing machines [44, 45]. So Differential needs a
model per product, built from the schematic, and trusts its hazard map (the points that can exceed 50 V) only once the
model’s healthy readings match the manual’s chart; until then every point of a high-voltage unit counts as high voltage.
“No single fault fits” covers modified units; units without a schematic are out of scope.
Labeled fault data is scarce, so we simulate it. Confirming a failed part takes unsoldering and is rarely recorded; shop
logs are private and, after recapping, unlabeled. We know of no public dataset of bench readings with confirmed failed
parts; published methods use benchmark filters [46] and simulated data [47]. Analog-test research built simulated fault
dictionaries [48], found faults the test points cannot tell apart (ambiguity groups) [49] and applied machine learning [50];
we follow it (Table 2). Other label sources fall short: faults built into real boards give true labels but each is built and
measured by hand, so they test transfer only; expert fault trees are one per product; forum threads and service bulletins
report fixes that worked, without readings. A simulated unit draws every part within its tolerance, the mains within ±5%
(our assumption), and one cataloged fault (an open, short, drift, or a mode such as a leaky transistor or worn tube). Since
units reaching a bench are old, 50% of training units are also aged: electrolytics up to 30% low with up to 3× the series
resistance (past the usual end-of-life limits of 20% and 2× [35]), resistors up to 10% high, tubes up to 30% weaker (our
assumptions). Each reading carries instrument error: a 10 MΩ meter loads each DC node [51] and reads within ±(0.5% +
2 counts) [52], an oscilloscope within ±3% of full scale [53]. The library holds 253,000 units (0 failed to converge). Hand

  Part 3 — Objectives and measurable success criteria                                                               Section lead: [name removed]

Objectives. (O1) Name the group (faults whose readings match within tolerance at every test point) containing the failed
part, on as-new and aged units; (O2) with less effort to a confirmed answer than scripted troubleshooting; (O3) with its
uncertainty shown: calibrated probabilities, ambiguity groups, an explicit “no single fault fits”; (O4) safely: no unsafe step
or invented reading; (O5) practically: fast, cheap, reading plain complaints and meter photos. Table 3 lists the primaries
and release gates; the secondary targets, reported but not deciding success, are (O1) T2 channel strip ≥ 94%, T3 in the top
three ≥ 99%; (O2) T8 effort ÷ chart’s ≤ 0.64×, T10 ÷ random’s ≤ 0.59×, T11 complaint read ÷ ignored ≤ 0.98×; (O3) T22 out-
of-catalog ≥ 51% flagged and AUROC ≥ 0.77, T23 single faults ≥ 94% named and ≥ 99% right when naming, T14 wrong
prior ≥ −1/−1 points; (O4) T24 powered readings above 50 V ÷ half-split’s ≤ 0.19×; (O5) T18 complaint-reading F1 ≥ 0.90
(Claude) and ≥ 0.80 (rules), T19 meter photos exact ≥ 95% (Claude) and ≥ 90% (offline), T20 p95 time per step ≤ 0.5 s, T21
API cost per diagnosis ≤ $0.10 (Claude parts need an API key).
Effort. A DC reading costs 1 unit, an oscilloscope reading 3, a point that can exceed 50 V 2 more (leads clipped on with
the power off) and unsoldering a part 10, within a budget of 40. Every method is charged one unsoldering test to confirm
its named suspect unless it already confirmed one. Every comparison is repeated with the scope, unsoldering and high-
voltage weights halved and doubled, and effort becomes bench minutes once the tickets and S1 give step times.

How it will be measured. Test sets: 5,000 single-fault units (2,500 channel strip, 500 per block) and 5,000 aged units with
the same mix, on seeds disjoint from training (aging within the training ranges); faults are drawn uniformly and kept
only on units that show a symptom, not by field failure rates. Complaints: from a phrase bank the complaint reader (rules
offline, the language model live) was not developed on (its overall score was seen once, before rules were extended from
development text only; T18 uses a sealed bank), in three voices (a bench technician, a hobbyist owner, a studio client),
never naming a part, each symptom dropped with probability 20% and others added with 2%. Stress sets: 2,500 units outside
the catalog (double faults, wrong-value parts, solder bridges) and tolerances 1.5-3 times wider (a curve). Baselines: a fixed-
order service chart, half-split tracing (halving the signal path; the strongest script) and random probing (never unsolders)
share the engine’s inference and stopping rule; the scripts unsolder their leading suspect at 40% (chart) or 50% (half-split)
belief, each tuned for them on the pilot; a language model, alone and with our simulator as a tool, picks from the same list
on 300 stratified units and stops when it names an answer; a healthy-channel comparison, usual in stereo gear, joins them
at M2 without a bar (it needs twin units on a shared supply). Real boards (T7): a teammate who does not record draws 60
buildable faults at random and inserts them in sealed order into three 24 V line-driver boards, one built from used parts;
each point is recorded once per fault, every method is replayed on the recordings, and results are reported per board and
beside simulation on the same faults. User study S1: six or more technicians timed with and without the tool, without a
bar (six cannot show a benefit reliably); its planted-error rule is gate T25.
How the bars were set; no test unit has been run. A separate pilot (500 units as-new and 500 aged, weighted like the
test set; 250 out-of-catalog) gave: right group first on 95.6% of as-new and 93.2% of aged units, a 2.4-point cost of aging (of
aged units, 5.2% flagged, 1.6% misnamed). To a confirmed answer, the engine alone spent 16.0 effort units and half-split
tracing 24.6; the engine asked for 0.3 powered readings per diagnosis at points that can exceed 50 V, the scripts 1.8 (T24).
Each bar is the pilot estimate moved 1.5 standard errors against the system (T1 1.0, T6 1.2, T13 2.0 points) and rounded,
never past the estimate, with one bar per part (T4); counting the pilot’s own error, each primary is met with a chance of
59-88%. T5 has no pilot (no API key): +10 points is the smallest margin we judged worth a per-circuit model. T7′s 70% is
what 60 faults can show: a pass needs 49 right, which a system truly 90% accurate reaches 99% of the time, an 85% one 82%
and a 70% one 3%, so a pass allows about a 10-point drop from simulation. Each primary is judged alone; if independent,
all six pilot-estimable primaries would pass together with a chance near 25%, about one missing.
Scope. In: single static faults (opens, shorts, drift, wear) and the symptoms they cause (no or low output, hum, distortion,
bass or treble loss, output DC) in supplies, preamplifier and line-level stages. Out: power amplifiers; hiss, intermittent,
thermal and mechanical faults; double faults (flagged); unpowered checks and visual inspection; consumer self-repair;
mains-side work; unattended probing. Plan (owner): interviews and ticket coding by M2, week 8 (Lead 1); by M3, week
11, the boards, T7 and one product’s low-voltage section transcribed and checked against its manual (Lead 2) and the full
evaluation (Lead 3); S1 and live runs by M5, week 13 (Lead 4). Late results stay pending, never replaced.
  DESIGN DECISION · Score by ambiguity group and effort to a confirmed answer
  Options: per-part accuracy and a count of measurements, or group accuracy and weighted effort to a confirmed answer.
  Choice: the latter: per-part scoring rewards lucky guesses between faults with identical readings, and an answer is worth
  acting on only once confirmed. Consequence: group sizes and raw effort are reported too.


  Part 4 — Preliminary AI approach, risks and ethics                                                Section lead: [name removed]

Why a hybrid. Language models guess when uncertain, partly because training and evaluation reward guessing [54].
Alone, a model has no expected readings for this unit under tolerance, no calibrated belief over hundreds of suspects
and no cost per measurement; with a simulator as a tool [19] it checks one guess at a time but lacks the last two (T5
and a model-with-simulator baseline test this). Simulation supplies the physics; Bayesian inference keeps a probability
for every suspect group, updated after each reading through likelihoods learned from simulated units: one to three full-
covariance Gaussians per fault (chosen by the Bayesian information criterion) over the circuit’s readings, so any subset
of readings has a closed-form likelihood. “No single fault fits” is a hypothesis with a broad flat density, set on as-new
validation units to flag at most 5% of single faults. Suspects start equal (the field’s fault mix is unknown); the complaint’s
symptoms reweight them through a symptom model learned from simulation; T14 tests a wrong prior. Boosted trees
[55] are a comparison engine (as-new pilot top-1 without the complaint: 91.8% against 94.3%). The next measurement
maximizes expected information gain per unit of effort, the one-step entropy rule of model-based diagnosis [56] with a
cost term (an optimal sequence needs a search [57]); a diagnosis stops at 90% belief in one group, at the budget, or when
nothing informative remains. The language model (Claude, version pinned) reads complaints (offline, rules do), proposes
meter-photo readings for confirmation (offline, image processing) and explains; every number it writes must trace to a
reading (T17), and it stays only if it beats a model alone (T5) and reads complaints and photos well (T18, T19).

Set aside: consistency-based candidates [56] (tolerances blur conflicts); retrieval over manuals (they describe healthy
units and common faults); deep networks that condition on any subset of readings [58, 59] and choose measurements by
information gain [60], but harder to audit than one density per fault. Autonomy: on five levels adapted from [61] (L0,
none, to L4, unattended), Differential is L2: it ranks suspects and recommends steps; a person takes every reading.
Safety. The hazard map covers healthy and faulted units, and a fault can put high voltage where there is normally none:
channel-strip point TP10, normally about 0 V, reaches 126 V under “C203 short circuit”. Steps at points above 50 V carry
hands-off and discharge warnings; in a high-voltage chassis every powered step says “high voltage inside”, names a meter
rated CAT II 600 V or better and waits until the technician records in a form how the unit was brought up (variac, series
lamp, current-limited supply, or already running). Before a part is removed, the owner’s approval and a reading under
2 V at every point that can hold high voltage are recorded; the check lapses after 5 minutes unless a bleeder is clipped
across the main filter capacitor, as a discharged capacitor recovers charge [35], and on any power-up. These are checks,
not guarantees: the locks act on the technician’s form entries, so they bound what the tool recommends and records, not
what a person does; the residual shock risk rests on the user’s unchecked qualification, and the hazard map covers only
the modeled circuit. It cannot yet recommend the unpowered checks bench practice starts with (resistance, diode, ESR).
People. Runner-up suspects and reasons are shown, yet AI can mislead: consultants given GPT-4 were 19 percentage
points less likely to solve a task outside its competence [62], and those trusting AI more report less critical thinking [63].
Trainees commit their own next step before seeing the tool’s and need a named supervisor near high voltage; a tutor
giving hints instead of answers largely mitigated the learning loss an unrestricted chat model caused [64]. S1 plants one
wrong recommendation on a non-hazardous step of a 24 V board, after the IRB’s determination; participants are told
beforehand that some advice may be wrong, are debriefed and recruited outside their employers (T25). Whether owners
turn faster diagnosis into a faster pace is a question for the interviews. As fairness audits do for groups of people [65],
errors are broken down by complaint voice, since owners who describe faults in plain words could get worse diagnoses
(non-English complaints are not covered; breakdowns by fault type show testability, not fairness): a voice more than 5
points worse on the T1 or T6 units, with an interval excluding zero, blocks release (T26).
Accountability. The team answers for the hazard map, stated performance and corrections; a shop, for its circuit models
and staff; the technician, for measurements and precautions. Responsibility should follow control, lest the technician
become a moral crumple zone, blamed for a system they cannot steer [66]: the repair ticket records the tool’s version, what
it recommended and why, and what the technician entered in the forms; it stays in the shop’s system, and the terms of use
forbid using it to rate, discipline or pay staff. Of the rules in Part 1, OSHA’s is met by selling to businesses and supervising
trainees, the AI Act’s by telling owners of the AI and forbidding staff rating, and the FTC’s by quoting only locked results,
labeled simulated or real. Whether advice software is a product (strict liability) or a service (negligence) is unsettled in
the U.S.: bills would make AI systems products [67, 68] or shield developers whose tools licensed professionals use, which
bench technicians are not [69]; the revised EU directive makes software placed on the market after 9 December 2026 a
product [70], and California bars defending a harm claim by saying the AI acted autonomously [71].
  DESIGN DECISION · Safety and security rules live in code, not only in the prompt
  Options: prompt the model to be safe, or enforce safety in code. Choice: code: prompt injection ranks first among LLM
  application risks [73] and injected text can steer a model [74], so notes, complaints and photo text are data, the model’s tools
  act only on the session and safety records come only from the forms. Consequence: the same warnings, refusals and locks
  in every mode.
