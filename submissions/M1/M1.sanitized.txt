MILESTONE 1 · PROBLEM STATEMENT

Differential: simulation-grounded AI diagnosis for analog
audio electronics
PRV 201 Frontiers of Technology (AI) · Track A: Build · Stevens Institute of Technology · September 2026


Executive summary. Analog audio equipment (tube preamplifiers, studio consoles, vintage hi-fi) is kept running by
bench technicians, and finding the failed part is the skilled, separately billed step: specialist shops we sampled charge
$125 to $150 for a bench evaluation [1, 2, 3]. Differential assists a qualified technician: for a modelled circuit (Figure 1) it
simulates every catalogued single-part fault under part tolerances, keeps a probability for every suspect, and recommends
and explains the measurement expected to rule out the most suspects per unit of effort. A language model reads complaints
and explains, but never supplies a reading the technician has not confirmed; safety rules run in code. Seven primary
targets, locked before the full evaluation (Table 3), test the claims: the failed part or its group of indistinguishable faults
named first in at least 93% of 1,000 simulated units; at least 8 points more accurate than a scripted chart and than
random probing, and 10 more than a language model alone; at most 0.35 times a chart’s measurement effort; calibrated
probabilities; and no broken safety or grounding rule. Scope: single static faults in preamplifier and line-level circuits.




Figure 1. Differential in one picture. Offline, a circuit model and its fault catalog are simulated with SPICE (standard circuit-simulation
software) under part tolerances and learned as fault signatures. Online, a Bayesian loop picks each measurement while the language model
            (LLM) only reads and explains. Safety rules and the check that every number traces to a measurement run in code.


  Part 1 — Problem, stakeholders and industry context                                                         Section lead: [name removed]

The problem. Studio consoles, tube preamplifiers and vintage hi-fi often outlive their makers. When one fails, a
technician must find which of dozens to hundreds of parts failed; that search, more than the repair, takes the skill and the
bench time. Differential is an assistant for that search.
Diagnosis is billed on its own. Three specialist shops we sampled (studio, vintage hi-fi, guitar and tube amplifier) charge
$125 to $150 for a bench evaluation, at one covering the first hour of work, at another credited to the repair [1, 2, 3]; a
studio-gear shop bills a one-hour minimum even when no fault is found [4]. These are single-shop examples: the share
of a bill that diagnosis takes is not published, so we will ask technicians.
A workforce that is hard to replace. The closest U.S. occupation, audiovisual equipment installers and repairers, covers
installation as well as repair; it employed 24,720 people in May 2023 [5] and is projected to grow faster than average
[6]. Vintage analog repair relies on a narrower pool of bench-trained specialists that formal programmes rarely produce;
many are near retirement and repair queues run to months or years [7], and one trade column reports that analog circuitry
is not taught at two-year colleges [8]. An AI assistant raised the productivity of 5,179 customer-support agents by 14%
on average and 34% for novices [9]; if that pattern holds at the bench, newly qualified technicians would gain most.

Parts wear in known ways, electrolytic capacitors losing capacitance and gaining series resistance as their electrolyte
evaporates [10], and tubes have been scarce since early 2022 [11], so replacing good parts on a guess is costly.
Where current AI stands. Maintenance AI retrieves documented knowledge for instrumented fleets and common
devices: PTC’s ServiceMax AI (2025) [12], Microsoft’s field-service Copilot [13], Siemens’s repair guidance (2025) [14],
IBM’s Maximo 9.2 agentic assistant (June 2026) [15] and iFixit’s FixBot (2025), drawing on 125,000 repair guides [16].
None of these models the circuit of a unit without sensors. At the bench the alternatives are the service manual’s voltage
chart, the unit’s healthy second channel, power-off signature testers, forums and chat models. The best chat model scores
95.50% on the September 2026 Razavi-Bench of textbook analog questions, as judged by other models [17], but a unit
on the bench differs: parts sit anywhere within tolerance, different faults give the same readings, measurements cost
effort, and some points carry lethal voltage. One system reached 97.59% on undergraduate circuit problems after adding
a simulation loop that flags mismatches [18]; Differential is built around such a check. Table 1 lists who is affected.

  DESIGN DECISION · The primary user is a qualified bench technician
  Options: a consumer self-repair assistant, or a professional bench tool. Choice: professional, because diagnosis often means
  testing a powered, open chassis. Under OSHA’s electrical rules, our benchmark, live parts are de-energised before work unless
  testing needs power, parts under 50 V to ground are exempt, and only qualified persons may work on parts that are not de-
  energised [19] or test circuits [20]. Our audit of every simulated unit found that 5 of the channel strip’s 23 test points can
  exceed 50 V, 4 in normal operation and 1 only under a fault (Part 4 — Preliminary AI approach, risks and ethics). Consequence:
  the interface assumes meter and oscilloscope skills, mains-side work is refused, and the tool will be offered to repair shops,
  not consumers.


  Part 2 — How diagnosis is done today, and the data landscape                                             Section lead: [name removed]

Today’s practice. The usual sequence, which our interviews will check, is to confirm the complaint, look for burnt or
bulging parts and check the supplies; follow a test tone stage by stage, often halving the search (“half-splitting”); compare
voltages with the service manual’s chart or the healthy channel; swap tubes; and finally unsolder a suspect part to test it
alone. What varies is judgement about which reading is really “off”, since every part sits somewhere within its tolerance
and old parts drift beyond it. When judgement runs out, a common fallback is replacing every electrolytic capacitor
(“recapping”), sometimes justified by age [10], sometimes a substitute for diagnosis.
The information is uneven. Service manuals with schematics and voltage charts exist for many classic models, but
units are often modified or undocumented. State repair laws require documentation only for digital products (New York
since 28 December 2023 [21, 22], Texas since 1 September 2026 [23]), and the EU’s obligation to repair covers only product
groups in its Annex II, such as washing machines and displays [24, 25]; vintage analog gear falls outside all of these.
What Differential needs from a unit. A circuit model: the netlist (the parts and how they connect), where each test
point is, and the operating conditions, taken from the service-manual schematic; we will time one transcription. Units
modified away from their schematic are what the “no single fault fits” outcome is for; units with no schematic are out
of scope.
Labelled fault data is scarce, so we simulate it. Confirming a failed part takes unsoldering and is rarely recorded in
structured form, shop records are private, and each model is a different circuit (Table 2). We know of no public dataset of
bench readings with confirmed failed parts; published fault-diagnosis methods are evaluated on benchmark filter circuits
[26] and simulated data [27]. The analog-test literature built simulated fault dictionaries [28], showed that some faults
cannot be told apart at the available test points, forming ambiguity groups [29], and later applied machine learning [26,
30] to drift faults that normal tolerance variation makes hard to detect [27]; we follow that path. A simulated unit is one
draw of every part within its datasheet tolerance plus one catalogued fault: an open, a short, a value drift, or a part-
specific mode such as a leaky capacitor or a worn tube. Five original blocks and a composite channel strip (59 parts, 250

  DESIGN DECISION · Original generic circuits instead of commercial schematics
  Options: model a commercial product from its service manual, or design original circuits with the same topologies. Choice:
  original circuits: a supply with a zener regulator and a 250 V tube supply, a 12AX7 tube stage, a passive bass-and-treble tone
  stack, an op-amp stage and a two-transistor line driver. Why: no copying of service documents, full ground truth, and the
  standard topologies of preamplifier and line-level gear. Consequence: transfer to real units must be shown, first on a low-
  voltage fault board of the driver block (T7), then by transcribing one documented product.


  Part 3 — Objectives and measurable success criteria                                                               Section lead: [name removed]

Objectives. (O1) Name the failed part or its ambiguity group accurately. (O2) Use less measurement effort than scripted
troubleshooting. (O3) Be honest: calibrated probabilities, visible ambiguity groups, an explicit “no single fault fits”
outcome. (O4) Be safe: no unsafe step and no invented reading. (O5) Be practical: fast, cheap, usable with plain-language
complaints and meter photos. Targets T1-T7 test O1, T8-T11 O2, T12-T14 O3, T15-T17 O4 and T18-T21 O5.
Effort units. Effort counts 1 for a DC reading, 3 for an oscilloscope measurement, 2 extra at a point that can exceed 50 V
normally or under a fault (hands-off measurement takes longer) and 10 for unsoldering a part to test it, within a budget
of 40 per diagnosis. The weights are estimates: every effort comparison is repeated with the scope, unsoldering and high-
voltage weights halved and doubled, and they will be timed on the fault board.

How it will be measured. The test set holds 1,000 single-fault units (500 on the channel strip, 100 per block), sampled
uniformly from simulated units that show a symptom, so each fault appears as often as it would reach a bench; seeds
are disjoint from training. Each unit gets a templated complaint in one of three voices (owner, musician, technician) that
never names a part; a separately written paraphrase bank tests the complaint reader (scored once, in aggregate, before the
rules were last extended from development templates only; both scores will be reported). Readings carry meter error of
±(0.5% + 2 counts) and oscilloscope error of ±3%; meter loading is not modelled. Further sets: 100 double-fault or modified
units, and 300 units with tolerances 1.5 times wider. Every unit is diagnosed by random probing, a fixed-order chart, half-
split tracing, the engine with each of two likelihood models, and the full system. On a stratified 300-unit subset a language
model works alone, and with our simulator as a tool (reported without a bar), seeing what a technician sees (complaint,
parts list, voltage chart, fault list, readings on request, the same budget), with a pinned model and a published prompt.
Also reported: accuracy by how often a fault causes a symptom; group sizes (on the channel strip, 251 hypotheses form
216 groups, 188 of size one); and misses split into wrong names and “no single fault fits” calls. A study of technicians
using the tool on the fault board, with and without it, is planned but not locked.
Pilots set the bars; no test unit has been run. Two earlier pilots exposed problems, since fixed: the “no single fault
fits” threshold was not yet calibrated, so the first (engine alone) scored 99% on the channel strip without ever using that
outcome; and in the second, measurement choice ignored that outcome, so some sessions stopped early. The bars come
from a third pilot, run after the fixes on units simulated for it alone (200 channel strip, 60 per block). On the channel strip
the full system named the group in 95.5% of units at a mean effort of 4.3, against 78.5% at 24.5 for the chart, 85.5% at 21.6
for half-split tracing and 82.5% at 23.4 for random probing, and 85.3% with wider tolerances. The “no single fault fits”
threshold is set on validation units to flag at most 5% of single faults; 25 of the full system’s 27 misses over all 500 pilot
units were such calls, the price of flagging 55% of double faults and modified units.
Scope and non-goals. In scope: single static faults (opens, shorts, drift, wear) and the hum and distortion they cause. Out
of scope: power stages (cascading and mains-side faults need another catalog); intermittent and mechanical faults and
random noise such as crackle (a static simulation cannot produce them); double faults (flagged, not diagnosed); mains-
side work (safety); units without a schematic (no model); probing without a technician.
  DESIGN DECISION · Score by ambiguity group and weighted effort
  Options: per-part accuracy and a count of measurements, or accuracy by ambiguity group and weighted effort. Choice: the
  latter. Some faults give identical readings at every test point; scoring per part would reward lucky guesses and punish honest
  ties, and counting measurements would treat unsoldering a part like touching a probe. Consequence: accuracy targets are
  group aware, group sizes are reported, and effort targets use weighted units.


  Part 4 — Preliminary AI approach, risks and ethics                                                  Section lead: [name removed]

Why a hybrid. Language models sometimes guess when uncertain, partly because training and evaluation reward
guessing over admitting uncertainty [31], and the 97.59% system above relied on a simulation check. So simulation
supplies the physics, and Bayesian inference keeps a probability for every suspect, “no single fault fits” included, updated
after each reading. The next measurement is the one with the highest expected information gain per unit of effort, as in
sequential fault diagnosis [32] and analog test selection [28]; a diagnosis stops when one group reaches 90% probability,
the budget runs out, or no measurement is informative. The LLM reads complaints (with a rule-based offline fallback),
proposes meter-photo readings for the technician to confirm, and explains each step from the engine’s outputs; every
number it writes must trace to a measurement or tool result (T17). Table 4 compares the options.
Autonomy. On a five-level scale borrowed from driving automation, from L0 (manual) to L4 (the tool probes alone), this
build works at L2, recommending while the technician measures and decides, and at L3 only on the 24 V fault board,
where it reads instruments over SCPI with approval.
Safety. A fault can put high voltage where there is normally none: in our audit of every simulated unit, channel-strip
test point TP10, normally at about 0 V DC, reaches 125 V under the fault “C203 short circuit”. Steps at any point that
can exceed 50 V carry hands-off measurement and discharge instructions; in a chassis with a high-voltage supply every
powered step carries a live-chassis notice; unsoldering stays locked until the technician enters a measured filter-capacitor

Over-trust and bias. Runner-up suspects and each step’s reason are always shown and every ticket needs the technician’s
sign-off, but explanations can also raise trust in wrong advice; the technician study will include a deliberately wrong
recommendation, and sampled explanations will be checked against the engine’s actual reasons. Old electrolytics do fail
often, but folklore blames them first; T14 tests that readings override a capacitor-heavy prior. Errors will be disaggregated
by circuit, part kind and fault type, as in audits of commercial gender classifiers [34]. Complaint reading is scored by
writer voice, but the voices are our templates; non-native writers and regional terms (“valve”, “earth”) stay untested until
the team collects real complaints.
Security and privacy. Prompt injection ranks first in the OWASP Top 10 for LLM applications [35], and LLM-integrated
applications blur data and instructions [36]; service notes, complaints and photo text are treated as data, and the model’s
tools act only on the diagnosis session. Offline mode sends nothing out; live mode sends complaint text and metadata-
free meter photos to the model provider.
Accountability, liability and regulation. The tool would first go to U.S. repair shops; EU law, like OSHA, serves as
a benchmark. Responsibility is allocated, not signed away: the team answers for the hazard map, stated performance,
warnings and updates; the shop for who uses the tool and for its circuit models; the technician for the measurements
and precautions taken. Beyond existing U.S. product-liability and negligence law, the proposed RISE Act would shield
developers who publish a model card and specification only when a “learned professional” uses the tool [37]; a bench
technician may not qualify, so we publish both for transparency, not as a shield. The revised EU Product Liability Directive
counts software as a product from 9 December 2026 and excludes damage to property used only professionally, not
personal injury, the harm that matters here [38]. The EU AI Act would not class a bench aid as high-risk: it is not a product,
or a safety component of one, that Annex I law subjects to third-party conformity assessment, and it falls in no Annex III
area; but Annex III covers evaluating workers’ performance [39], so our intended use excludes grading technicians. From
2 August 2026 people must be told they are dealing with an AI unless it is obvious [39, 40]. Table 5 maps controls to NIST
AI RMF functions [41].

  DESIGN DECISION · Safety rules live in code, not only in the prompt
  Options: instruct the model to be safe, or enforce safety in code. Choice: code; the prompt repeats the rules but cannot
  override them, since injected text or user pressure can talk a model out of an instruction [36]. Consequence: warnings,
  refusals and the lock behave the same in every mode, and T15 tests them exhaustively.

Appendix A · Industry watch
 • Frontier models score highly on textbook analog questions (September 2026). The top Razavi-Bench model scored 95.50%
   (judged by other models), and the benchmark has an experimental simulator-assisted mode [17]. Grounding, calibration and safety
   are what a bench tool must add; T5 tests that.
 • IBM Maximo 9.2 (June 2026) adds an agentic assistant and an MCP server for customers’ own agents [15], a possible route for a
   diagnosis engine into industrial repair depots.
 • EU AI Act transparency rules apply (2 August 2026) [40], while the Digital Omnibus moved the Annex III high-risk rules to 2
   December 2027 [42]. They would bind Differential only once offered in the EU.
 • Repair rights widen for new products. EU repair rules began to apply on 31 July 2026 [43] and Texas’s law took effect on 1
   September 2026 [23]; both help newer digital products, not vintage analog gear.
 • Claude Sonnet 5.5 released (28 September 2026) [44]. Models change quickly, so Differential pins its model, caches responses
   and re-runs its safety and grounding targets on any change.
