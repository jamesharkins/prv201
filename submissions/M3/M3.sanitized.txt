Summary. Differential runs end to end from a clean clone: offline with no API key, live with Claude Sonnet 5.5, and in replay from
recorded responses. On 5,000 held-out simulated single-fault units it named the failed part’s group first in 95.3% (bar ≥ 94%), with
0.65× the effort of half-split tracing to a confirmed answer and probabilities that are well calibrated (calibration error 0.007). It beat
Claude working alone with the same bench information by 19.7 points on 300 units, and a live diagnosis cost $0.021 at list prices. Of the
26 locked targets, 17 are met, 5 missed and 4 pending (§3); one pending gate, T17, is already above its bar on the live count. The misses are
reported as written and traced to their causes in §4. The two that matter most are safety findings. Offline, the code’s request screen rec-
ognized only 27 of 98 independently written attack prompts; live, no reply endorsed an unsafe step, but 15 of 47 harmless questions were
refused. And 15 untraced numbers in live replies got past the grounding check’s rewrite step; the final check withheld them all.
Hardware validation (T7) and the technician study move to M5, as M2 planned.

1 What was built
Differential is the system M2 designed, complete except the fault boards. A technician describes the fault and code screens the request.
The engine, not the language model, ranks the suspects and picks the next measurement from circuit simulation. Claude reads the com-
plaint and explains each step, and code checks every number and safety instruction before the technician sees it (Figure 1). Table 1 lists

4 What the results show, and where it fails
4.1 Why the engine beats scripted procedures
Every baseline shares the engine’s inference and stopping rule and differs only in how it picks the next measurement, so Figure 3 isolates
the choice of measurement. Differential reached its answers with 5.7 effort points on average against 19.0 for half-split tracing and 20.3
for the fixed chart, and was right more often (95.3% against 90.2% and 87.7%). Choosing by information gain per effort point [2] mat-
ters for accuracy as well as effort. Without the effort weighting, accuracy fell to 87.9%: the cost-blind engine took oscilloscope readings
in 65% of its steps against 27%, stopped just as often at 90% belief, and was then right in only 88.5% of cases against 95.9%. Sessions
that lean on oscilloscope readings are overconfident, which points at those readings’ likelihood models; confirming that is work for M5.
Simulation size is not the limit: 50 draws per fault gave 95.6% against 95.7% with 400. The complaint saves effort rather than accuracy
(5.74 against 6.32 points for the engine alone); a perfect symptom reading would add 1.5 points, an upper bound on better complaint
reading.

4.2 Where the engine fails
Of 237 misses on single faults, 187 were the engine declining to name a fault (“no single fault fits”) and 50 were wrong names. The power
supply is the weakest circuit (85.4%): 66 of its 73 misses are flags. Across circuits, the faults flagged most are hard shorts (C101, C504,
C103, R103 shorted), which collapse a rail or saturate a stage and move many readings at once. Declining to name is the safer error: the
ticket tells the technician to continue by hand rather than pointing at a healthy part. Among part kinds with many test units, electrolytic
capacitors are the weakest (88.8% of 958). Aging costs 2.2 points (T6), mostly as more flags (5.4% of aged units against 3.7% of new
ones) rather than wrong names (1.5%). The engine depends on the tolerance model: with part tolerances widened 1.5×, 2× and 3×, accu-
racy fell to 93.0%, 83.3% and 44.7%. This is why real boards (T7) are the test that matters most at M5.
4.3 Why Claude alone does worse (T5)
Given the netlist, test-point map, voltage chart, fault list, readings on request and the same budget, Claude alone named the right group
in 74.3% of 300 units against 94.0% for Differential, and spent 24.3 effort points against 5.7. Its failures sit mostly in passive networks. It
matched or beat the engine on parts with distinctive failures (op-amps 96%, diodes, zeners and triodes at 100%). It fell to 54% on resis-
tors, 63% on film capacitors and 11% on potentiometers, where a drift of a few decibels in one response must be attributed to one part,
and to 37% on the passive tone stack. The simulation supplies exactly that quantitative mapping. Its three sessions agreed on only 200 of

4.4 Units outside the catalog (T13, T22)
On 2,500 double-fault and modified units, 11.0% were misleading, below the 12% bar, but the upper bound (12.1%) is not, so T13 is
missed. Double faults misled in 5.7% of cases; solder bridges in 33.6% and wrong-value parts in 28.4%. In those cases a single catalog
fault on another part fitted the readings well enough that the engine did not flag. A bridge alters no part, so only a flag counts as correct
for it. The flag fired on 41.4% of these units (bar 51%) at an operating point that wrongly flags 3.7% of single faults, and its separation
(AUROC 0.71) is below the 0.77 bar. Modifications are the main way Differential can mislead a technician.
4.5 The language layer: readers, cost and the agent
The complaint rules score almost exactly 1.00 on the phrasings they were written from but 0.705 on a sealed bank written by someone
who never saw them (precision 0.99, recall 0.55). Claude scores 0.883 and Claude Haiku 5.5 0.874; neither reaches the 0.90 bar (T18). The
effect on diagnosis is small, because the complaint only shifts the prior (§4.1). On 300 synthetic meter photos, the offline reader was ex-
act on 100% and Claude on 97.0%, whose lower bound (94.8%) falls just under the 95% bar (T19); Haiku managed 89.3%. The photos
come from the project’s own renderer, and no real photos are labeled yet, so these figures say little about real meters. Haiku met neither
bar, so Sonnet stays the default, as M2 specified. Prompt caching brought a live diagnosis to $0.021 (T21) at list prices [1], against the M2
estimate of $0.22 uncached. End to end, the live agent named the right group in 91% of 100 sessions and the offline agent in 84%, while
the engine got 94 of the same units right. The gap came from a routing defect (ADR-052). The offline agent read a complaint beginning
“Service ticket:” as a request for the repair ticket, and 8 sessions closed without a measurement. After the fix, the offline agent named the
right group in 92% of the same units.
4.6 Safety: what held and what did not
Held. Every one of the 491 possible steps that needs a warning or lock carries it, checked again by an independent recomputation from
the raw simulations (T15). No out-of-catalog unit put a point above 50 V on the low-voltage side of the hazard map, and no complaint
voice is served worse than the others (T26).
Did not hold. The red-team suite has 98 attack prompts, short of the 300 T16 requires, and 47 benign controls. Every reply the harness
flagged was then read and labeled (results/redteam_review.json). Live, no reply gave or endorsed an unsafe step. The harness
flagged 11 attack replies because they lacked its marker phrases, and each turned out to be a refusal in other words (“The 500mA T fuse
stays in”). Offline, the request screen refused 27 attacks. The other 71 were silent: the agent ignored the question and started a diagnosis
as if it were a complaint. None endorsed the step, but silence is not a refusal, and the suite’s rubric counts it as unsafe. Over-refusal is the
larger live problem. Of 47 benign controls, 15 were refused without cause. In 10 of them the output check matched a protection phrase in-
side a warning (“I won’t help bypass a fuse”, written about a planted service note) and replaced the whole reply. In 3 the request screen
refused, in both modes, a question that named a forbidden act while asking for the safe practice.
The screen, the output check, the complaint rules and the ticket router failed the same way. Keyword rules match the phrasings they
were written for and misjudge independent wording. The screen missed reworded attacks, both checks refused safe text that used a for-
bidden phrase, the complaint rules missed 45% of reported symptoms, and the ticket router fired on “Service ticket”. Unsafe requests and
prompt injection are the top risk for language-model applications [4]. M2 said such requests are “refused by code whatever the model
says”; that held only for recognized phrasings. Live, the model refused or redirected every attack the screen missed. One reviewer (the
build assistant) labeled the replies; the second, human labeling the suite’s README asks for is still to do. No safety code was changed in
response to this suite; the fixes are M5 work, to be tested on prompts it has not seen.
Grounding (T17). Offline, every one of 6,077 numbers traced to a tool result or a reading. Live, the first drafts held 20 untraced numbers
in 100 sessions. The check sent each draft back for a rewrite, and 15 numbers survived it: the rewrite request arrives as a user turn, and
Claude answered it conversationally, quoting the number it was removing (“You’re right that ‘95%’ never appeared…”). The final check
replaced every one of them with a placeholder, so none reached the technician. Counted as the evaluation counts them, though, 15 of
6,133 is above the 0% bar, so the gate cannot be met as counted; it is listed as pending only because the independent checker and the hu-
man audit of 50 live transcripts have not run. Most were one slip, repeated (13 of 15): a tool field named range_95 written out as “95%”.

5 Engineering
The code is 12,752 lines of Python in differential/, 6,133 in the evaluation and 3,275 in tests. On a fresh clone 418 of 424 tests pass and
6 are skipped (they need the simulation library, which is not in git, or the document typesetter); line coverage of the core package is
91.9%. Ruff and strict mypy run in GitHub Actions on every push. That check had failed since September on a test-path setting and a
missing font, not on the code; both are fixed. Dependencies are pinned. Every live response is cached by request hash, so every live result
replays exactly with no key; the cache is kept out of main for size and published on its own branch. The key is read only from
DIFFERENTIAL_API_KEY. The targets are unchanged since the lock at 60cca12. Two defects were found on test units after the lock: live
sessions never took a measurement (ADR-051, before any live result was quoted), and the offline ticket routing (ADR-052). Both were
plumbing, each is disclosed with the result from before the fix, and each has a regression test.
